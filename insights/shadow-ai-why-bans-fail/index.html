Shadow AI: why bans fail and what to write instead

Shadow AI is AI staff use without approval. Bans push it out of sight. What to write in your AI policy instead, and how to find the tools already in use.

Someone in your organisation pasted a customer email into a public AI assistant this week to get a faster reply drafted. They did not ask anyone, because nobody told them they had to, and the reply was better than the one they would have written.

That is shadow AI: AI tools used for work without the organisation knowing or approving. It is not rare. In a 2026 survey reported by Help Net Security, 65% of decision-makers said they use AI tools their organisation has not approved, and nearly a third of employees said they would keep using AI tools even if they were banned.

Why bans fail

A ban is an easy policy to write and a hard one to keep. People use unapproved AI because it saves them time on real work. When the approved route is “no”, the work does not stop; it moves to personal accounts, personal phones and browser extensions, where nobody can see what data goes in. The organisation ends up with the same use and less visibility.

A ban also says nothing useful. It does not tell people which tools they can use, what they can put into them, or what to do when they need something new. So it is ignored the first time it gets in the way.

What to write instead

A policy people follow answers five questions in plain words.

Which AI tools are approved, and for what? A short list, by type of task, kept current.

What data may go in, and what never does? Customer personal data, credentials, confidential contracts and source code are the usual “never” list. Say it as a list, not as a principle.

Who checks the output? A person reviews anything AI produced before it is sent, published or used to decide something.

How do I ask for a new tool? A route that answers in days, not months. If asking is slower than ignoring the policy, people will ignore it.

What happens if I make a mistake? Someone who reports that they pasted the wrong thing into an AI tool should be thanked, not punished. Otherwise the next mistake is hidden.

The free Brivellis Acceptable Use Policy template covers these points: its AI-assistant section sets the approved tools, the data that never goes in and a person's review of every output; its worked example shows a request route for new services answered within days; its reporting section says people who report their own mistakes are thanked, not punished; and its acknowledgement form records who read which version, and when.

Then find what is already in use

Writing the policy does not tell you what people already use. That needs the list from Ep 1 of our AI Governance Basics series: an AI inventory. Look in software admin consoles for AI features that are switched on, in renewals and expense claims for AI subscriptions, in browser extensions and meeting note-takers, and ask three teams a short question: which AI tools do you use, for what, and with what data? Add each one to the inventory, then decide: approve, replace or stop.

Where ISO/IEC 42001 comes in

ISO/IEC 42001 requires an AI policy set by top management (5.2) and a defined scope for the AI management system (4.3). An organisation with significant shadow AI cannot show either is true in practice: its real AI use is outside both. The steps above are how most organisations close that gap (common practice, not wording from the standard). On the security side, ISO/IEC 27090 treats what goes into an AI system as a threat surface, one more reason to know which systems your data is going into.

Do this this week

Ask three teams the three-question survey. Write down every tool they name. Then compare the list with the tools your policy mentions.
