ISO/IEC 42001 implementation: what the standard asks for, in the order you'll build it

How to implement ISO/IEC 42001 step by step: find your AI, set scope and policy, assess risk and impact, build the Statement of Applicability, then check.

Most organisations asked to “do ISO/IEC 42001” already use AI. What they lack is a list of it, a way to judge its risks and its effects on people, and a record that shows an auditor the two are connected.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 asks for an AI management system (AIMS): a set of decisions and records that keeps the organisation’s use of AI under control, the same way an ISMS does for information security. The requirements are not long, but the order you build them in decides whether the system holds together. This article walks through that order, with the worked example for Falcon Ridge IT Services, a fictional company, from the free AIMS Starter Kit for ISO/IEC 42001:2023, and points to the kit file for each step. It is a practical route into AI governance, not a summary of the standard: requirements are paraphrased and cited by clause number, and anything that is our own method is labelled as such.

Step 1: Find the AI before you write anything

The standard does not use the word “inventory”, but it does require you to set the boundaries of the AIMS (4.3), and you cannot draw a boundary around systems you have not found. In practice, auditors now expect a list behind the scope statement.

Most AI in an organisation was never deployed as a project. It arrived inside tools people already use: ticket triage in the service desk, transcription in the meeting suite, screening features in the HR platform, public chat assistants people brought in on their own. In the kit’s worked example, Falcon Ridge, a managed service provider, finds six AI systems in a week. Only one had ever been called an AI project.

Kit file: 02 AI System Inventory.

Step 2: Context, roles and scope (4.1–4.4)

Next, write down what affects your use of AI (internal and external issues, including climate, which the 2023 edition already asks you to consider), who has a stake in it, and your role towards each AI system. An organisation that builds a model carries different duties from one that only switches on a feature in software it licenses. The scope then names which systems, sites and activities the AIMS covers, and states any exclusions with a reason.

Kit file: 01 AIMS Context, Roles and Scope.

Step 3: AI policy, objectives and roles (5.2, 5.3, 6.2)

The standard requires an AI policy set by top management (5.2), assigned roles and responsibilities (5.3) and AI objectives you can measure (6.2). Keep the policy short and specific to how your organisation uses AI. Each objective needs an owner, a target, a measure and a date, or it cannot be evaluated later.

Kit file: 03 AI Policy, Objectives and Roles.

Step 4: One method for risk and impact (6.1.2–6.1.4)

This is where ISO/IEC 42001 differs most from ISO/IEC 27001. It requires an AI risk assessment (6.1.2) and risk treatment (6.1.3), and also an AI system impact assessment (6.1.4): a look at the possible consequences of an AI system for individuals, groups of people and society, not only for the organisation.

Two questions, then. Risk asks what could go wrong for the organisation. Impact asks who outside it could be affected, and how. The kit uses one method for both, with the same 5×5 scales as the Brivellis ISMS, BCMS and SMS kits and one added impact area for individuals, groups and society (a Brivellis method, not prescribed by ISO). Falcon Ridge’s CV-screening feature is the system that gets the completed impact assessment: it affects job applicants who never agreed to be scored by software.

Kit files: 04 AI Risk and Impact Assessment Method, 05 AI Risk Register and Treatment Plan, 06 AI System Impact Assessment.

Step 5: The Statement of Applicability (6.1.3)

Risk treatment produces the Statement of Applicability: every control in Annex A (38 in the 2023 edition), whether it applies, why, and how it is implemented. Built after the risk work, each line is a consequence of a decision. Built first, it is a wish list. Exclusions need a justification an auditor can challenge.

Kit file: 07 Statement of Applicability (controls by number, with our own short descriptors — read the control titles in your licensed copy).

What the auditor asks

  • Which AI systems sit behind this scope statement?
  • Which risk or impact decision led to this control?
  • Why is this control excluded?

Step 6: Operate and keep the evidence (7.5, 8)

Clause 8 asks you to run what you planned: carry out the risk and impact assessments again when things change (8.2–8.4), and control the AI system’s life cycle. Retraining on new data, a new use for an existing model or a supplier’s new feature are all triggers. Documented information has to be controlled (7.5). In practice, an evidence register that lists, for each applicable control, the record that proves it is what turns a policy into something an auditor can test.

Kit files: 08 AI System Life-cycle Record, 09 Documented Information and Evidence Register.

Step 7: Check, review, correct (9.2, 9.3, 10.2)

An internal audit programme (9.2), a management review (9.3) and corrective action for nonconformities (10.2) close the loop. Falcon Ridge’s example audit raises two nonconformities, and the management review records what changes because of them.

Kit file: 10 Internal Audit and Management Review Pack.

The thread that holds it together

Pick one AI system and follow it: inventory, risk tier, impact assessment, risk treatment, Statement of Applicability row, evidence. We call this the integrity chain (a Brivellis teaching model, not prescribed by ISO). If the thread breaks anywhere, the audit will find the break. The kit’s example contains one deliberate gap, an applicable control with no evidence yet, so you can practise finding it.

If you already run ISO/IEC 27001

You can reuse most of the management-system clauses: both standards share the same high-level structure. The kit’s crosswalk (file 11) maps ISO/IEC 42001 clauses to ISO/IEC 27001:2022 clauses, Annex A to the NIST AI RMF 1.0 functions, and ISO/IEC 27090 threat categories to the risk register columns. It is a Brivellis mapping, not an official equivalence.

Where AI helps, and where it must not decide

An AI assistant can draft an inventory entry from a product description or suggest risks for a new use case. It must not set risk scores, reach impact conclusions, decide applicability or create evidence. The kit’s prompt pack (file 12) labels every output DRAFT, and the AI-use log (file 13) records what was assisted and who reviewed it.
