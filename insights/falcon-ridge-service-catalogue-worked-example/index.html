Worked example: a service catalogue an auditor can trace

A fictional worked example: how Falcon Ridge turned an eleven-line list of “services” into an ISO/IEC 20000-1 service catalogue an auditor can trace.

Worked example

The problem

Falcon Ridge IT Services had a service catalogue. It was a spreadsheet tab with eleven rows, and it had grown the way most catalogues grow: one row added whenever someone sold, built or renamed something. Some rows were services a customer received. Some were internal activities (“patching”, “backups”). One was a team.

The weakness showed up in a practice internal audit. The auditor took the scope statement, picked the Managed Infrastructure service (SVC-003) and asked to see it in the catalogue, in its service level agreement, in the supplier agreement for the colocation data centre behind it, and in last month’s incident records. It appeared under three different names across those four places. Of the other ten catalogue rows, three had no service level agreement at all, and two were not services anyone outside the company would recognise.

None of this was a failure of effort. It was a catalogue built as a list instead of as a record.

What Falcon Ridge built

The team rebuilt the catalogue around one rule: a row is a service a customer receives, or it is not in the catalogue. Internal activities moved to the processes that need them. The team moved to the organisation chart, where it belonged.

Each remaining service got the same columns: an ID that never changes, one name used everywhere, a short description and the outcome it gives the customer, a service owner, the customers who receive it, service and support hours, an availability target, the key dependencies and suppliers behind it, the service level agreement reference, and a status. A second rule followed from the first: a name changes in the catalogue first, and the catalogue owner tells the SLA and supplier-agreement owners the same day.

The result was a shorter catalogue: four rows instead of eleven, each one traceable. The three live services match the scope statement word for word. The fourth, a security monitoring pilot, is marked as in the pipeline and stays outside the scope until a management review brings it in.

Before and after, for one service. Before: “Hosting and network” in the catalogue, “Data centre services” in the SLA, “Colocation and hosting” in the supplier agreement, and a free-text category in the incident tool. After: “Managed Infrastructure (SVC-003)” in all four, with the SLA reference and the colocation provider listed on the catalogue row itself, and the incident tool’s service field fed from the catalogue list rather than typed by hand. Nothing about the service changed. What changed is that anyone, an auditor included, can now follow it from one document to the next without asking which name means what.

The kit files it used

The rebuild used the Service Catalogue tab of the SMS workbook (file 04) in the free SMS Starter Kit for ISO/IEC 20000-1, which has those columns. The kit’s worked-example workbook (04b) holds Falcon Ridge’s finished catalogue. Each row’s SLA reference points to an agreement built from the service level agreement template (03), with a completed one for Managed Microsoft 365 in 03b, and the worked scope statement (01b) names the same three services.

The kit’s AI prompt pack (05) has a prompt that drafts one catalogue row per service in those columns. Falcon Ridge pasted in its old eleven-row list as well and asked the assistant to mark which rows were services, which were activities and which were teams. It used the answer as a draft: the service owners made every decision, and the AI-use log (08) records what the assistant suggested.

The result

On the re-run of the trace, the auditor picked a different service and followed it from the scope to the catalogue, the service level agreement, the supplier agreement and the incident records. The name matched in all five places, and the trace took minutes instead of an afternoon. Every live service in the new catalogue has its own service level agreement; the rows that never had one were the activities and the team, and they are gone from the catalogue for good.

Run the same trace on your own catalogue: pick one important service and count how many names it has across your documents. If the answer is more than one, start there.
