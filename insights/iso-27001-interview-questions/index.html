ISO 27001 interview questions: 12 questions for audit and GRC roles, and how to answer them

Twelve ISO 27001 interview questions for audit and GRC roles: what the interviewer is checking, a strong answer, and a free way to build a work sample.

If you are interviewing for an ISO/IEC 27001, GRC or IT audit role, the questions are more predictable than they feel. Interviewers want to know three things: do you understand how an information security management system (ISMS) fits together, can you apply it to a real situation, and can you explain it plainly to someone who is not a specialist.

Below are twelve ISO/IEC 27001 interview questions that come up for analyst, implementer and internal auditor roles, grouped the way most interviews run. For each one: what the interviewer is checking, and the shape of a strong answer. Requirements are paraphrased and cited by clause number; the answers are common practice, not wording from the standard. If you want the auditor's side of the same ground, our companion piece covers the ten questions auditors ask your team.

1. “What is ISO/IEC 27001, in one minute?”

They are checking whether you can explain it without jargon. A strong answer: it is an international standard for running an information security management system, a set of policies, risk decisions and controls that an organisation operates, measures and improves. Organisations can be certified against it by an independent body. Mention the current edition, ISO/IEC 27001:2022, and its 2024 amendment (ISO/IEC 27001:2022/Amd 1:2024), which adds climate change as something to consider under 4.1 and 4.2.

2. “What is the difference between ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO/IEC 27002?”

A classic. ISO/IEC 27001 sets the requirements an organisation is certified against; ISO/IEC 27002:2022 gives guidance on the controls. Strong candidates add that Annex A of ISO/IEC 27001 lists the controls, and that the organisation decides which it needs through its risk treatment (6.1.3).

3. “Why does the standard start with context and scope?”

They want to hear that the management system comes before the controls. Clause 4 asks the organisation to work out what surrounds it, who has a stake, and where the boundary sits (4.1 to 4.3). A strong answer: you cannot choose the right controls until you know what you are protecting, for whom, and how far the system reaches.

4. “Walk me through a risk assessment”

They are testing method, not vocabulary. Describe a repeatable process (6.1.2): agree criteria and scales, identify risks to confidentiality, integrity and availability, assign an owner, analyse likelihood and impact, evaluate against the acceptance level. Then treatment (6.1.3): choose an option, select controls, and get the owner's acceptance of what is left.

5. “What is a Statement of Applicability, and how do you build it?”

The strong answer puts it after the risk work, not first: it follows from the risk treatment (6.1.3). It records the controls you need, why each is included, whether it is in place, and why any Annex A control is left out. In practice most teams list every Annex A control. Weak answers describe it as a checklist.

6. “How many controls are in Annex A of the 2022 edition, and how are they organised?”

A knowledge check. 93 controls in four groups: governance and process ones, ones about people, physical ones and technical ones. Bonus: the 2022 edition merged and added controls compared with the 2013 edition, so a transition was needed for existing certificates; that transition closed on 31 October 2025.

7. “What makes an internal audit objective?”

Clause 9.2 expects the people who audit to have no stake in the work they check. A strong answer: nobody audits their own work; plan by risk and previous results; sample real records; grade findings consistently; report to management.

8. “What is the difference between a correction and a corrective action?”

A correction fixes this instance; a corrective action removes the cause so it does not happen again (10.2). Strong candidates add the step most people forget: checking later that the action actually worked.

9. “What happens in a management review?”

Clause 9.3: top management reviews the ISMS on a set schedule using defined inputs, such as audit results, progress on objectives, nonconformities and changes, and makes decisions. The tell of a strong candidate: "the output is decisions and actions, not minutes that note a report."

10. “A team says a control ‘doesn't apply to us’. What do you do?”

They are testing judgement. Ask which risk would need it, check legal and contractual requirements, and if it truly is not needed, record a justification that would survive an auditor's question, with a date to look at it again.

11. “You find a nonconformity a week before the certification audit. Hide it or log it?”

Log it. Show the correction, the cause, the corrective action and the plan to check it (10.2). An auditor trusts a system that finds its own problems more than one that claims to have none.

12. “How would you use AI in ISMS work?”

A newer question. A balanced answer: AI can draft documents and turn a Statement of Applicability into practice questions, but a competent person reviews every output, it never invents evidence or records, and confidential data stays out of tools the organisation has not approved. Mentioning ISO/IEC 42001:2023 as the AI management system standard shows range.

How to prepare: bring a work sample

Reading answers is not the same as having done the work. A small, honest work sample shows what you can do. First, take a free template kit and complete it for a fictional company: a scope statement, five risks, and the matching Statement of Applicability lines. Second, follow one risk all the way through to the evidence and an audit question, so you can talk about it end to end. Third, practise explaining it in two minutes to someone outside security.
