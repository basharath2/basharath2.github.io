NIST CSF 2.0: what it is, how organisations use it, and where to start

A plain-language guide to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0: how it is built, how organisations use it, what people often get wrong, and the free Brivellis templates that map to it.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is a set of cybersecurity outcomes, published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, that any organisation can use to understand, assess, prioritise and communicate how it manages cybersecurity risk. It describes what good looks like; it does not prescribe how to get there.

The current version is CSF 2.0, published on 26 February 2024 as NIST CSWP 29. It replaced version 1.1, added a sixth function, Govern, and widened its stated audience beyond critical infrastructure to organisations of every sector and size. NIST keeps the supporting material, such as informative references, implementation examples and quick-start guides, online and updates it more often than the framework itself.

What it is, and what it is not

It is a framework of outcomes. Each outcome says what should be true, not how to make it true. You decide the method, the tools and how far to go.

It is risk-based. Organisations choose which outcomes matter for them, and in what order, using profiles. Two organisations can both use the CSF well and have quite different targets.

It is not a certification scheme. NIST has said it has no plans to develop a conformity assessment programme for the framework. There is no official CSF certificate; an assessment against it produces a report, not a certification.

It is not law in itself. NIST describes use of the framework as voluntary. It becomes a requirement when a contract, a regulator or a government policy refers to it; US federal agencies, for example, are directed to use it.

Who it applies to

Any organisation, of any size and in any sector, that wants a shared language for cybersecurity risk between technical teams, management and the board. In practice it is used most by organisations that report to boards or customers in CSF terms, by suppliers whose customers ask for a CSF profile, and by organisations that already run ISO/IEC 27001:2022 and want an outcome view on top of it.

How the framework is built

CSF 2.0 has three parts: the Core, a list of outcomes grouped into functions, categories and subcategories; Organizational Profiles, which describe a current or target state against the Core; and Tiers, which describe how rigorous an organisation's cybersecurity risk governance and management are. The Core has 6 functions, 22 categories and 106 subcategories. In our own words:

NIST CSF 2.0 functions and their categories, summarised in our own words

FunctionWhat it covers, in short
Govern (GV)Set, communicate and oversee the cybersecurity risk strategy, expectations and policy, including roles and supply chain risk. 6 categories (GV.OC, GV.RM, GV.RR, GV.PO, GV.OV, GV.SC), 31 subcategories
Identify (ID)Understand current cybersecurity risk: assets, risk assessment and what needs to improve. 3 categories (ID.AM, ID.RA, ID.IM), 21 subcategories
Protect (PR)Use safeguards: identity and access, awareness and training, data security, platform security and resilient infrastructure. 5 categories (PR.AA, PR.AT, PR.DS, PR.PS, PR.IR), 22 subcategories
Detect (DE)Find possible attacks and compromises through monitoring and analysis of adverse events. 2 categories (DE.CM, DE.AE), 11 subcategories
Respond (RS)Act on a detected incident: manage, analyse, communicate and contain it. 4 categories (RS.MA, RS.AN, RS.CO, RS.MI), 13 subcategories
Recover (RC)Restore what an incident affected and communicate during recovery. 2 categories (RC.RP, RC.CO), 8 subcategories

Govern sits across the other five: it sets the direction they work to. The subcategory counts are the outcomes listed in the Core, which NIST notes is not exhaustive. Online, each subcategory links to informative references, such as other NIST publications, and to implementation examples.

The four Tiers run from Partial (Tier 1) through Risk Informed (Tier 2) and Repeatable (Tier 3) to Adaptive (Tier 4). NIST describes them as a measure of how rigorous risk governance and management are across the organisation, and encourages moving to a higher Tier when risks or mandates are greater, or when a cost-benefit analysis supports it. In our reading, a Tier describes the organisation, or the part of it a Profile covers, not a score for each outcome; NIST also gives examples of applying Tiers to selected Functions.

How organisations use it

  1. Set the scope: the whole organisation, a business unit, a system or a service.
  2. Write a Current Profile: for each relevant outcome, what is in place today and how well it works, with evidence.
  3. Write a Target Profile: the outcomes you want, with priorities that reflect your risks, obligations and budget, and the Tier you aim for as an input. A Community Profile for your sector or technology, where one exists, can be a starting point.
  4. Compare the two and turn the gaps into an action plan with owners, dates and the evidence that will show progress.
  5. Carry out the plan, update the Current Profile as outcomes improve, and repeat the cycle as risks change.

NIST describes a five-step flow for creating and using Profiles (scope, gather information, create the Current and Target Profiles, analyse the gaps and plan, implement and update); the steps above follow it in our own words, with the Tiers used as an input to the Target Profile. It is a suggested way of working, not a requirement. Because there is no certificate, the output is a profile, a gap list and a plan that you share with management, customers or auditors.

How it relates to other frameworks

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (information security): a certifiable management system with a risk process and 93 reference controls in Annex A. Many organisations run ISO/IEC 27001 as the system and report CSF outcomes from it. The free ISMS kit includes a crosswalk between the two (a Brivellis mapping, not an official equivalence).

NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 (security and privacy controls): a detailed control catalogue. The CSF is the outcome layer; SP 800-53 is one source of controls that can achieve the outcomes.

ISO 22301:2019 and ISO/IEC 20000-1:2018: the Respond and Recover outcomes overlap with business continuity and service continuity. A business continuity management system can supply the recovery priorities those outcomes are measured against.

Common misreadings

“We are CSF certified.” There is no NIST certification. A third party can assess you against the CSF and write a report, but that is not a certificate.

“We must achieve every subcategory.” Profiles exist so that you choose. A Target Profile that marks some outcomes as low priority, with reasons, is normal.

“Tier 4 is the goal.” Tiers describe rigour, and the right Tier depends on your risk. A higher Tier where the risk does not justify it is cost without benefit.

“The CSF replaces ISO/IEC 27001.” They answer different questions. ISO/IEC 27001 is a certifiable management system; the CSF is a way to describe and communicate outcomes. They work well together.

“Govern is just policy.” Govern covers context, risk strategy, roles, policy, oversight and supply chain risk, not only policy documents. It is where CSF 2.0 changed most from version 1.1.

Frequently asked questions

Is NIST CSF 2.0 mandatory?

Not for most organisations. NIST describes use of the framework as voluntary. It becomes a requirement when a contract, a regulator or a government policy refers to it.

Can we be certified to NIST CSF 2.0?

No. NIST has said it has no plans for a conformity assessment programme. You can be assessed against the CSF and share the report, or be certified to a standard such as ISO/IEC 27001 and map that to the CSF.

What changed from version 1.1?

Version 2.0 added the Govern function, widened the audience beyond critical infrastructure, moved supply chain risk management into Govern, added implementation examples, and keeps them and the informative references online so they can be updated more often.

What is a profile?

A description of cybersecurity outcomes for a defined scope. A Current Profile says what you achieve today; a Target Profile says what you want to achieve and in what order. A Community Profile is a shared baseline for a sector, technology or threat.

How many outcomes are there?

The Core lists 106 subcategories in 22 categories under 6 functions. Not all will matter equally to you; the Target Profile is where you set priorities.

Do we need ISO/IEC 27001 to use the CSF?

No. The CSF can be used on its own. Organisations that already run ISO/IEC 27001 often find that their ISMS covers many CSF outcomes, and use a crosswalk to show where.
