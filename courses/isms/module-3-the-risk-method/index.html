Risk criteria and scales that give consistent results

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

The risk method is the document auditors test hardest. Clause 6.1.2 asks for a risk assessment process that produces consistent, valid and comparable results. Consistent is the key word. Open file 03, the Risk Assessment and Treatment Method. It starts with the scales. Likelihood runs from one, rare, not expected in ten years, to five, almost certain, several times a year.

Impact runs from one to five, scored on money, service, legal position, reputation and personal data, using the worst credible effect in any of them. The score is likelihood times impact, so one to twenty-five. Twelve or more is High: treat it, and only top management can accept what's left. Seven to eleven is Medium: treat it, or accept it with a written reason from the risk owner.

Six or less is Low: accept it, and keep watching. Why such precise scales? Because two workshops, run by different people, should reach similar scores for similar risks. Vague words like "likely" or "significant" give scores that depend on who's in the room. Anchor every level to something observable: a frequency, a number of records, hours of downtime. Clause 6.1.2 also asks for risk acceptance criteria.

Falcon Ridge keeps them in one table: who can accept which level, and what must be written down when they do. That table settles arguments before they start. Three things auditors check here. Scales that are defined but not used, so scores in the register don't match the method. Impact judged on one dimension only, usually money. And no named risk owners.

The clause asks you to identify risk owners, so every risk needs a person, not a department. Your turn. Adapt the two scales to your organisation's size, then set your thresholds and your acceptance rule. Test them on three risks you already know about. Next: running the assessment itself.

Identify, analyse and evaluate information security risks

Now the assessment itself. Clause 6.1.2 asks you to identify risks to the confidentiality, integrity and availability of information within your scope, analyse them, and evaluate them against your criteria. The kit's method lets you choose your approach. Asset-based: list your information assets, and ask what could go wrong with each. Or scenario-based: start from events, like ransomware on hosted servers, or a phished administrator.

The 2022 edition doesn't force either. Falcon Ridge uses scenarios. Open the risk register, file 04. Each row has an asset or scenario, the threat, the vulnerability, a named owner, likelihood and impact. The score and level calculate. Look at ISR-01: ransomware encrypts hosted servers and their backups, because the backup network isn't separated. Likelihood four, impact five: twenty, High.

How do you run it? Prepare a scenario list in advance. Hold short workshops with each risk owner. Score with the method open on the screen. And record why, not just the number. Falcon Ridge ran five workshops, one with each head of function, between February and early March. Clause 8.2 adds timing. Assess risks at planned intervals, and whenever there's a significant change: a new service, a new supplier, a major incident.

And keep the results. A register dated eighteen months ago, with no changes since, invites a finding. Falcon Ridge's first assessment found twenty risks, thirteen of them High before treatment. That's normal for a first assessment. It isn't a sign of failure. It's the reason the ISMS exists. Your turn. Draft ten scenarios for your scope, then score three of them with a risk owner, using the method.

Record the reason behind each score. Next: deciding what to do about them.

Risk treatment options, controls and acceptance

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

You've scored your risks. Now decide what to do. Clause 6.1.3 asks you to choose treatment options, determine the controls you need, compare them with Annex A, produce a Statement of Applicability and a treatment plan, and get the risk owners' approval. There are four options. Treat: reduce the risk by adding or improving controls. Avoid: stop the activity. Share: pass part of the risk on, through insurance or a contract.

Accept: knowingly keep it, within your criteria. Most of Falcon Ridge's risks are treated. One, unauthorised entry to the Westbrook field support office, is accepted, because it's already within the acceptance level. For a treated risk, list the controls it needs by Annex A number, up to four per row, or your own control IDs. ISR-02, account takeover through phishing, lists control 8.5, strong sign-in; 6.3, security awareness and role-based training; 5.17, protecting sign-in secrets; and 8.23, managing access to websites.

The comparison with Annex A is a safety net, not a shopping list. Design the treatment first. Then check the ninety-three controls to make sure nothing necessary is missing. If a control you hadn't considered would reduce a risk, add it. The Treatment Plan tab turns controls into actions: what, who, when and status, with an overdue flag. Falcon Ridge has twenty actions.

Fourteen are done, and one, scanning scripts for exposed secrets, is overdue. The flag makes sure nobody can pretend otherwise. Then score the residual risk, and get the owner's acceptance, with a date. A residual High needs top management. Falcon Ridge brought every High risk down to Medium or Low, and its risk owners accepted the register in March. Your turn. For three risks, choose an option, list controls by number, add actions with owners and dates, and score the residual risk. Next: cloud and AI risks, handled with the same method.

Cloud and AI risks in your ISMS risk register (ISO/IEC 27090)

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Two topics come up in almost every ISMS project now: cloud and AI. Both are risks you score and treat in the same register, mostly with controls you already have. Start with cloud. Every cloud service splits security between you and the provider. The provider secures its platform. You still own your accounts, your configuration and your data. Incidents happen in the gap, where each side assumes the other has it.

Falcon Ridge records this as ISR-09: cloud services, with data exposed or lost through a gap in who secures what. The split with each provider isn't written down. Likelihood three, impact four: twelve, High. The treatment, TP-05, is a shared-responsibility matrix for each provider, reviewed yearly. Residual: eight, Medium. In the Statement of Applicability, control 5.23, security when buying, using and leaving cloud services, is partial.

The office suite matrix is drafted; the recovery site's is due at the end of July 2027. Partial, with a date and an owner, is an honest status. Two companion standards go deeper. ISO 27017, whose second edition came out in 2026, follows the 2022 control structure and adds a few cloud-specific controls. ISO 27018 covers personal data in public clouds.

Both are guidance alongside your ISMS. Your certificate is still against 27001. Now AI. It reaches your ISMS in three ways: people using AI tools at work, AI features inside products you already buy, and AI systems you build or run yourself. For the first two, write scenarios such as confidential data pasted into an unapproved AI tool, or a supplier's new AI feature processing your data without review.

The controls are already in Annex A: 5.10 for rules of use, 5.12 for classification, 5.19 to 5.23 for suppliers and cloud, 8.12 for stopping sensitive data getting out, and 6.3 for awareness. Falcon Ridge's example has no AI row; add your own. If you build or run AI systems, look at ISO 27090, published in October 2026. It's guidance on threats to AI systems, such as poisoned training data, inputs crafted to fool a model, model theft and prompt injection, with ways to detect and reduce each.

Use it as a scenario checklist. Governing AI as a whole is a different job, covered by ISO 42001. Your turn. For one cloud service, write down who secures what. Then add and score one AI scenario. Next, Module 4: the full register and the Statement of Applicability.
