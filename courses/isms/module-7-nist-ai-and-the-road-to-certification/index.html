ISO/IEC 27001 to NIST CSF 2.0: one control set

Lesson 7.1 · 2:242 minutes 24 seconds

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Your board, your customers or your partners may speak a different security language: the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, version 2.0. One set of controls can answer both. Here's how. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 organises outcomes into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover, with twenty-two categories beneath them. It's published free by NIST. It isn't certifiable, but it's widely used to describe security posture.

File 08 maps every clause and all ninety-three controls to CSF categories. For each control you'll see our categories, and the best-match subcategory from the informative reference for ISO 27001, 2022, listed in NIST's online catalogue. We compared our mapping with that reference. Sixty-four controls already matched at category level. For the other twenty-nine, we added the reference's category and kept ours.

So the crosswalk is aligned with the reference, and a little broader. It's still a Brivellis mapping, not an official NIST or ISO one. How do you use it? Filter by category, read the status from your Statement of Applicability, and you have a CSF view without a second assessment. When someone asks how strong your Detect function is, the answer is already in your ISMS.

The CSF Categories tab counts how many clauses and controls map to each category. Thin spots tell you where your ISMS says little in CSF terms: a useful question for your next risk assessment. Your turn. Filter the crosswalk for one function, say Detect, and read your status for every control in it. Next: tailoring the kit with AI, safely.

Tailor ISMS templates with AI, safely

Lesson 7.2 · 2:262 minutes 26 seconds

Plays from YouTube (privacy-enhanced mode). Google's privacy policy applies.

Watch on YouTube (opens YouTube in a new tab) →

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

AI can save you days of drafting. It can also invent facts, cite the wrong clause, and expose data you never meant to share.

Here's how to keep the time saving and lose the risk. The kit's Responsible AI Use Guide, file 00a, sets six rules. A person decides. Protect data. No invented evidence. Check accuracy. Be transparent. And be fair and accessible. File 09, the prompt pack, starts with a master context you fill in once: your organisation, your scope, your systems in generic terms, your laws and your roles.

It tells the assistant never to quote the standard, never to invent scores, status or evidence, and to label every output DRAFT. Then come eight prompts, one per job: context issues, the policy, risk scenarios for workshops, Statement of Applicability wording from links you supply, evidence ideas, audit questions, a management review summary, and a mock auditor's review of any document.

Notice what the prompts never ask for. Risk scores. Control status. Evidence. Audit results. Sign-offs. Those come from your people and your records. An ISMS built on invented evidence fails the first sample an auditor takes, and deserves to. Record each session in the AI-use log, file ten: the date, the document, the tool, whether the data check passed, who reviewed it, and what they corrected.

Falcon Ridge's reviewers dropped seven of twenty-six suggested risk scenarios, and corrected eleven justifications. That's the review working. Your turn. Fill in the master context, run the risk-scenarios prompt, and review every scenario before it reaches a workshop. Then log the session. Next: the capstone, and your road to certification.

Capstone and your road to ISO/IEC 27001 certification

Lesson 7.3 · 2:372 minutes 37 seconds

Plays from YouTube (privacy-enhanced mode). Google's privacy policy applies.

Watch on YouTube (opens YouTube in a new tab) →

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

You've built the documented core of an ISMS. Is it ready? Let's check it, then map the road to a certificate. The capstone has three parts, plus a short AI-check at the end: your scope statement, your risk register with at least ten scored risks, and your Statement of Applicability with all ninety-three rows decided. Use your own organisation, or Falcon Ridge.

Score yourself with the capstone checklist attached to this lesson. You upload nothing. Here's what good looks like. A scope an auditor can test in one reading. Every risk with an owner, a score from the method and a decision. Every applicable control with a justification, a status and an evidence item. Every exclusion with a reason. And no red checks.

Then five steps. One: run the ISMS for at least three months, producing records. Two: complete an internal audit and a management review. Three: choose an accredited certification body. Four: Stage 1, the documentation and readiness review. Five: Stage 2, evidence that it runs. Choose a certification body accredited for ISO 27001 by a recognised accreditation body. Get at least two quotes, and compare audit days, not just price.

Ask how they'll sample your Annex A controls. At Stage 2, three traps catch many first-timers. Records that start the month before the audit. A management review with no decisions. And a Statement of Applicability that nobody in operations recognises. You've built answers to all three. One last habit. At each management review, check whether ISO has published any amendment to 27001, and use only official copies of the standard.

Remember the blank page? You now have an ISMS core you can run, measure and defend. Well done, and good luck with your audit.
