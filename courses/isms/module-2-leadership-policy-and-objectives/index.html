Transcript

An ISMS without top management is a project, not a system. Clause five asks leaders to show commitment, set a policy, and assign roles.

Here's what that looks like on paper, and in practice. Open file 02, Information Security Policy and Objectives. Part A is the policy itself: a short statement of commitments, the principles behind it, a register of topic-specific policies, roles and authorities, and how the policy is communicated. Clause 5.2 expects a policy that suits the organisation's purpose, includes your objectives or a framework for setting them, and commits to meeting applicable requirements and to continual improvement.

The template covers each one in plain language. Keep yours short. Two pages of commitments people remember beat ten pages nobody reads. The top-level policy doesn't try to say everything. Detailed rules live in topic-specific policies: access, backup, acceptable use, supplier security and so on. The register lists each one with an owner, an approver and a next review date.

Auditors sample from it, so keep it current. Then roles. At Falcon Ridge, the chief executive approves the policy and accepts high residual risks. The information security manager owns the ISMS. Risk owners own their risks. And every manager makes sure their teams follow the rules. Clause 5.3 wants these assigned and communicated, including who reports on ISMS performance to top management.

How do you prove clause 5.1? Not with a signature alone. Auditors look for decisions: a budget approved, a risk accepted, a management review that changed something. Collect that evidence as you go. Your turn. Tailor the policy, list your topic-specific policies with owners, and fill the roles table. Then ask one question: who in top management will sign this, and will they recognise the commitments as their own? Next: objectives you can actually measure.

Security objectives you can measure

"Improve security" is not an objective. Clause 6.2 expects objectives that fit the policy, are measurable where practicable, are monitored and communicated, and come with a plan. Let's write some that survive an audit. Part B of file 02 holds the objectives table. Each row has a measure and a target, what will be done, the owner, the due date, the resources, and how the result will be evaluated.

Those columns aren't decoration. They're what the clause asks you to plan. Falcon Ridge sets five. Multi-factor sign-in on every account by the end of April. Critical fixes applied within fourteen days for at least ninety-five percent of findings. At least ninety percent of treatment actions closed by their due date. Awareness training completion of ninety-five percent. And ready for the certification audit by the end of November.

Where do good objectives come from? Mostly from your risk register. Falcon Ridge's first objective exists because account takeover was one of its highest risks. If an objective doesn't link to a risk, a requirement or the policy, ask why it's there. Then measure them. Each objective names its evidence: a monthly coverage report, a patch report, the treatment plan itself.

When management review comes round, the question isn't "did we try?". It's "what does the evidence say?" One more requirement, new in 2022: clause 6.3. Changes to the ISMS must be planned. A new site, a new service or a new supplier shouldn't just happen. A good habit is to record the purpose, the possible effects, the resources and the owner before you make the change. Your turn. Write three to five objectives, each with a measure, a target, an owner and a due date, and link each one to a risk or a requirement.
