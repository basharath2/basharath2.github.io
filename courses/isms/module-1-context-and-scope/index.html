Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Before you can protect information, you need to know what shapes the risks around it. Clause 4.1 asks you to determine the internal and external issues relevant to your ISMS.

Here's how to do it without writing an essay. Open file 01, Context and Scope, at the issues table. Each issue gets an ID, a one-line description, whether it's internal or external, its effect on information security, and where it's addressed. That last column is the one auditors test. Falcon Ridge lists five. C-01, internal: every hosted application runs in one third-party data centre.

C-02, internal: two senior agents hold most of the service desk know-how. C-03, external: phishing and ransomware aimed at managed service providers. C-04, external: the business units and the insurer want evidence of controls. C-05, external: summer heat peaks stress power and cooling.

Here's the test. An issue that leads nowhere is decoration. Every issue should point to a risk in the register, an objective, or a requirement you've decided to meet. Falcon Ridge's single data centre becomes risk ISR-04. The insurer's demand becomes a requirement. If you can't trace an issue, either it isn't relevant, or your risk register is missing something.

Now the 2024 amendment. The next table asks one question: is climate change a relevant issue for this ISMS? Answer yes or no, and record the reason. Then check whether any interested party has climate-related requirements. Falcon Ridge says yes: heat peaks threaten the data centre's cooling, so the issue is addressed by a risk. Three common findings here. Issues copied from a generic list, with no link to anything.

No climate decision at all, which since 2024 is an easy nonconformity to avoid. And issues that are never reviewed. Revisit them at every management review, and whenever the business changes. Your turn. List four to six issues for your organisation, both internal and external. For each, write the effect in one line, and point it at a risk, an objective or a requirement.

Then record your climate decision, with its reason. Next: interested parties, and what they actually require.

Who has a stake in your ISMS, and what do they need from it? Clause 4.2 expects three answers: the relevant interested parties, their requirements, and which of those requirements your ISMS will address. In file 01, the interested parties table has one row per party: who they are, what they require, whether that requirement is legal, regulatory or contractual, and whether the ISMS will address it: yes, partly or no, with a reason.

Falcon Ridge lists six. The group's business units, with confidentiality and availability commitments in their service levels. The data protection authority, with breach notification deadlines. Staff and contractors. Suppliers, such as the colocation operator and the carriers. The cyber insurer, who makes multi-factor sign-in and offline backups a condition of cover. And the parent board, who wants a quarterly security report.

Watch what happens to the insurer's condition. It doesn't stay in this table. It becomes a justification for two controls in the Statement of Applicability: strong sign-in, and backups. That's the point of clause 4.2. Requirements flow down into controls, and an auditor can follow them. The 2022 edition added a small but important question: which of these requirements will the ISMS address?

You don't have to meet every wish of every party. But if you choose not to, say so, and say why. A silent gap looks like an oversight. A recorded decision looks like management. Keep the detail somewhere it can be maintained. Laws, regulations and contract clauses change. The template points to a register of legal, regulatory and contract requirements, reviewed at least once a year, so this section stays short and current. Your turn. Name five to seven interested parties.

For each, write the two or three requirements that matter, classify them, and decide whether your ISMS addresses them. Then pick one requirement and find the control it justifies. Next: drawing the boundary of your ISMS.

ISMS scope, interfaces and processes

Your certificate will quote your scope word for word, so it's worth getting right. Clause 4.3 asks you to set the boundaries and applicability of the ISMS, taking account of your issues, your requirements, and the interfaces with others. In file 01, the scope section is built in four parts. What's in, by service, site and organisational unit. The interfaces and dependencies.

What's excluded, and why. And finally, the scope statement itself. Falcon Ridge's scope covers its three live services and the information it holds for them, its Northport head office and operations centre, the colocation data centre, the Westbrook office, and remote working. A security operations pilot is excluded for now, with a reason. Interfaces are where scopes fail audits. The colocation operator runs power, cooling and physical entry.

The cloud providers secure their own platforms. The business units own their own data. Each interface needs a line saying who is responsible for what, and which control covers it, often supplier controls and an assurance report reviewed every year. A quick test for any scope statement. Could an auditor tell, from the words alone, whether a given system, site or team is in or out?

If the answer is "it depends", tighten it. And don't scope out awkward areas just to make the audit easier. Auditors notice, and so do your customers. Clause 4.4 asks you to establish the ISMS as a set of interacting processes. The processes table, later in the same file, lists each one with an owner, inputs, outputs, the kit file and the clause.

It's your map: when someone asks how the ISMS works, this table is the answer. Your turn. Draft your in-scope list, your interfaces with their responsibilities, and any exclusion with its reason. Then write a two-sentence scope statement, and run the auditor test. Next, Module 2: leadership, policy and objectives.
