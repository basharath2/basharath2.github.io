Documented information: what you must write down

Lesson 5.1 · 2:162 minutes 16 seconds

Plays from YouTube (privacy-enhanced mode). Google's privacy policy applies.

Watch on YouTube (opens YouTube in a new tab) →

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

What must you write down? Less than people think, but what you do write must be controlled. Clause 7.5 covers documented information: what the standard requires, what you decide you need, and how you control it. Open file 06, the Documented Information and Evidence Register, at the Required Documents tab. Sixteen items the standard asks you to document, by clause, in our wording.

From the scope and the policy, through the risk method, the Statement of Applicability and the treatment plan, to audit results, management review results and corrective actions. Those sixteen are the minimum. Clause 7.5.1(b) adds everything else you decide is necessary for the ISMS to work: procedures for key processes, an incident response plan, topic-specific policies. Decide deliberately, and keep the list short.

Control means each document has an ID, a version, an owner, an approval and a review date; that it's available where it's needed; and that it's protected. The Document Register tab does exactly this for Falcon Ridge's fifteen controlled documents, and flags any review date that has passed. One distinction saves a lot of pain. Documents say what you intend to do: policies, procedures, plans.

Records prove what you did: logs, reports, minutes, tickets. Documents need version control. Records need protection from change, and a retention period. Your turn. Fill the Required Documents tab with your own document IDs and locations, then list your controlled documents with owners and review dates. Next: making every control auditable, with an evidence register.

The evidence register: answering "show me"

Lesson 5.2 · 2:202 minutes 20 seconds

Plays from YouTube (privacy-enhanced mode). Google's privacy policy applies.

Watch on YouTube (opens YouTube in a new tab) →

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

An auditor won't take your word for it. For each applicable control, they'll say: show me. This lesson builds the index that answers "show me" in under a minute. The Evidence Register tab lists every evidence item: an ID, what it is, the owner, where it lives, which controls cite it, when it was last reviewed, how often it's reviewed, and when it's next due.

Falcon Ridge has fifty-five items, from the policy library to backup restore tests. The register is an index, not a store. Point to the system or library where each record lives. Don't copy records into a spreadsheet: they'd escape their own access rules and retention periods, and go stale the day you copy them. The Evidence by Control tab closes the chain.

Paste the applicable and evidence columns from the Statement of Applicability, and every control shows OK, no evidence reference, or not in the register. Falcon Ridge's only red row is control 5.7, the gap we planted. The register also watches time. Evidence item ISE-038, the storage media handling records, was last reviewed in May 2026, on a twelve-month cycle. At the end of June 2027, it shows review overdue.

That's the second finding the internal auditor raises. What makes good evidence? It's dated. It's produced by the process, not written for the audit. It covers a real period, not just last week. And it's easy to sample: a report or an export beats a screenshot. Your turn. For ten applicable controls, write the evidence item, its owner and its location.

Then run the Evidence by Control check. Next: competence, awareness and communication.

Competence, awareness and communication

Lesson 5.3 · 2:302 minutes 30 seconds

Plays from YouTube (privacy-enhanced mode). Google's privacy policy applies.

Watch on YouTube (opens YouTube in a new tab) →

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Controls are run by people. Clauses 7.1 to 7.4 ask for resources, competence, awareness and communication. They're short clauses, and frequent sources of findings. Competence means deciding what each role that affects security needs to know and do, closing the gaps through training, mentoring or hiring, and keeping evidence. Not just certificates: a training record, a supervised task, an assessment.

Awareness is different. Everyone working under your control should know the security policy, how their own work contributes, and what happens if they don't follow the rules. Auditors test this by asking people, not by reading documents.

So ask your own people first. If you need a full programme for this, the free Brivellis Cybersecurity Awareness Programme Kit was built for exactly these clauses, and for control 6.3. Falcon Ridge uses it. Its awareness completion reached 96.7 percent in the second quarter, and that figure feeds the ISMS management review. Communication asks you to decide what to communicate, when, to whom, and how.

A one-page plan is enough: policy updates to all staff, risk reports to top management, incident notices to customers and authorities, each with an owner and a channel. And clause 7.1, resources: the budget and time to run the ISMS. Auditors look for it in management review decisions. If your ISMS owner runs it in spare hours, write that down, and decide whether it's enough. Your turn. List the roles that affect security, with the competence each needs and the evidence you hold.

Then ask three colleagues the three awareness questions, and note their answers. Next, Module 6: measuring, auditing and reviewing.
