Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Every recovery time in your BCMS should trace back to one place: the business impact analysis, the BIA, clause 8.2.2. Get it right and the rest of the system almost writes itself. Get it wrong and every plan is built on a guess. For each activity, the BIA answers four questions. If it stops, how fast does the damage grow?

When does it become unacceptable? So how fast must you recover? And what do you need to restart it at a reduced level: people, systems, suppliers, sites and records? Start with activities, not systems. Falcon Ridge lists six, from Identity, email and collaboration to field support in Westbrook. Each has one owner, a location, operating hours and a peak period, because a disruption during month-end close hurts more than one on a quiet Tuesday.

Who sets the numbers? The activity owners score the impact, in a short workshop. Top management agrees the impact scale, and the level it calls unacceptable, once, on the Settings tab. And the rationale column records who said so, and when. That's what makes a target defensible. The scale matters more than people think. Level four has to mean the same thing to every owner.

At Falcon Ridge, level four is major: for example, payroll or month-end close at risk, or a contract breach. That's the level its top management calls unacceptable.

Your turn: list the activities in your scope with one owner each, and book thirty minutes with top management to agree the impact scale and the unacceptable level. Next: how impact over time turns into MTPD, RTO and RPO.

MTPD, RTO and RPO explained with one curve

Lesson 2.2 · 2:422 minutes 42 seconds

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Three numbers drive every continuity plan: the MTPD, the RTO and the RPO. People often pick them out of the air. In this lesson you'll see how one simple curve gives you all three, in the right order. For each activity, score four impact categories, financial, operational, legal and reputational, at six intervals: one hour, four hours, one day, three days, one week and two weeks.

Falcon Ridge scored identity and email in an activity owner workshop in March. Now take the worst score at each interval and draw it as a curve. Impact rises over time. The maximum tolerable period of disruption, the MTPD: in the kit, it's the first interval where the curve reaches the unacceptable level. For identity and email, that's level four at one day.

The recovery time objective, the RTO, is how fast you commit to restoring the activity. It must land before the MTPD, with room for things going wrong. Falcon Ridge set eight hours for identity and email, well inside one day. The recovery point objective, the RPO, looks the other way: how much data, measured back from the moment of the outage, the activity can afford to lose.

Identity and email can tolerate four hours; the RPO is one hour. One note: RPO is good practice. ISO 22301 itself doesn't require it. You don't do this maths by hand. On the BIA Summary tab, the MTPD and the tier calculate from your scores. You enter the RTO, the data-loss tolerance and the RPO, and the RTO check and RPO check columns tell you straight away if a target doesn't fit.

Your turn: score one of your own activities at all six intervals, read its MTPD, then set an RTO and an RPO that pass both checks.

Next: the dependencies that decide whether you can actually hit those targets.

BIA dependencies: people, systems, suppliers, sites

A recovery target is a promise. Whether you can keep it depends on things you might not control: a supplier, a cloud platform, a key person on holiday. This lesson is about writing those dependencies down before a disruption finds them for you. The Resources and Dependencies tab asks, for each activity: the minimum staff at a reduced level, the key skills, the systems, the suppliers, the sites, the vital records, the equipment, and the workaround while systems are down.

Minimum, not normal. What's the least you need to keep going? Falcon Ridge's service desk can run on four of its twelve agents and one shift lead. Its ticketing runs on a software-as-a-service tool that the vendor recovers, not Falcon Ridge. It relies on remote working, with Westbrook as a second site. And if the tool is down, the workaround is email and phone intake with a paper log.

The dependencies people forget are the ones that hurt. Your supplier's suppliers: the cloud platform underneath a software-as-a-service tool. Sign-in, which every recovered system needs. The one person who knows how something works. And break-glass access, if nobody has tested it. For every activity ask: what does this depend on, that you don't control? This is a good place for AI.

Prompt B turns an owner's notes into an activity description and a dependency list, and leaves MTPD, RTO and RPO as to-confirm. When Falcon Ridge used it, the tool still proposed recovery times. The reviewer logged it in entry FRAIL-013, and they were deleted.

Your turn: fill the dependency row for your most critical activity, and circle every dependency you don't control. Those circles become the risks and strategies in Module 3. Next: the five BIA mistakes auditors find most often.

Five business impact analysis mistakes auditors find

Lesson 2.4 · 2:152 minutes 15 seconds

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Most BIA findings are the same five mistakes. The good news: you can test for all of them before an auditor does, and the workbook catches several automatically. Mistake one: an RTO that is not below the MTPD, which means you'd recover after the damage is already unacceptable. Two: an RPO above the data-loss tolerance. Three: targets with no source, where nobody can say who set them or when.

Watch the workbook catch the first one. Change the RTO for application hosting from twelve hours to thirty-six. The MTPD is one day, so the check turns red: RED FLAG, RTO not below MTPD. The flag stays visible until someone fixes the target. Mistakes four and five are about process. Four: scoring at an average time instead of the peak period, so month-end close or the payroll run is missed.

Five: a BIA that was never signed, or never reviewed after the organisation changed. The Sign-off tab fixes both. Each activity owner confirms their scores and targets, the analysis is approved by the Chief Executive Officer, and the next review is set twelve months out. Falcon Ridge also records that AI helped with descriptions only, under log entry 013.

Your turn: run all five tests on your own analysis. Fix every red flag, add a source to every target, rescore at peak, and get it signed. Then copy the Export to 04 block, because Module 3 checks your recovery options against these numbers.
