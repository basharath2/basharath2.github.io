Business continuity plan activation: the first hour

When something breaks, the first hour decides most of what follows. Who declares a disruption? Who gets called? Who decides whether to activate the plan? The continuity plan, file 05, answers those questions before anyone has to improvise them. Section two lists the triggers. A site is unavailable. A Tier one activity is down, or expected to be down, for longer than half its RTO.

A critical supplier can't deliver. Or a cyber incident threatens availability, in which case you contain first. Each trigger names who decides, and the first action. Authority must be unambiguous. At Falcon Ridge, the Chief Executive Officer declares activation and stand-down. If they can't be reached, the BCMS owner acts as deputy, and tells them as soon as possible. One person declares, one deputy covers, and everyone knows both.

Section three is the first hour. In the first fifteen minutes, confirm what's affected, open the incident log and call the team. By thirty minutes, decide: activate the plan, or manage it as an incident, and record the time. By forty-five minutes, assign recovery leads and send the first staff message. By the hour, check each activity against its RTO, and start customer notice if it's due.

The call tree works by role. Call the role holder, and if they don't answer within ten minutes, call the deputy. No phone numbers in the plan itself: they live in a secure contact list, checked every quarter, and within two weeks of any change. Out-of-date contacts are one of the most common exercise findings.

Your turn: write your triggers with a decider for each, name your declaring role and deputy, and adapt the first-hour table.

Next: what you tell staff, customers and suppliers, and how you look after people in a long disruption.

Crisis communication and people in a disruption

In most disruptions, the technical recovery isn't what people remember. They remember whether anyone told them what was going on. Section five of the plan sets who hears what, from whom, and when. Staff hear what happened, what to do, where to go and when the next update comes, within sixty minutes of activation. Customers hear which services are affected and the expected restore time, within the notice period in each agreement.

Suppliers hear what you need. Regulators hear only where a legal duty applies. And the media gets a short, approved holding line. The holding line is drafted and approved before it's ever needed. It says you're responding to a disruption, that the safety of your people and the continuity of service come first, and when the next update will be.

No speculation about causes, and no promises you can't keep. Plans are carried out by people. The Head of People owns welfare and safety, rotas, and staff messages. In a long disruption, rotas matter: nobody makes good decisions twenty hours in. And check the plan is fair and accessible. Does it rely on staff without a company phone, or staff who can't travel to a second site?

AI can help here. Prompt E drafts an activity procedure from your notes, keeps the RTO and RPO exactly as given, and adds placeholders for contact roles instead of names. It's also good at first drafts of holding statements and staff messages, for approval by the people who will send them.

Your turn: complete the communication table for your five audiences, draft your holding line and get it approved, and check your procedures for anyone they leave out. Next: the IT disaster recovery runbook.

IT disaster recovery plan: runbook and recovery order

File 06 is the IT disaster recovery runbook. One runbook, not one per system. Systems are grouped into four classes, recovered in a fixed order, and every step ends with a checkpoint and the evidence to record. The four classes are identity, network, core applications and data. Each takes its tier and RTO from the BIA, and a system takes the tier of the most time-critical activity it supports.

If it supports two activities, the shorter RTO wins. Then the recovery order. Network first, because everything else needs it, and it has the shortest RTO. Identity second, because nobody can sign in to a recovered system without it. Then the data restore, before the applications that need it. Then core applications, the service desk, and field support last. Each class has steps.

For core applications: confirm the latest verified restore point, fail over the Tier one virtual machines to cloud recovery, run the application checks in order, and finish with a checkpoint. The business owner confirms the application, and the time is recorded against the RTO. A step without evidence hasn't been done. Technology helps most where people struggle under pressure. Automated failover removes manual steps.

Orchestration scripts the recovery order. And a recovery-readiness view shows, at any time, which systems could meet their targets today; only about a third of organisations in a recent survey had one. Falcon Ridge's own test brought its Tier one systems back in nine hours twenty minutes, inside a twelve-hour target.

Your turn: sort your systems into the four classes, take their tiers from your BIA, and write your recovery order with a reason for each position. If you use prompt F, it must mark missing steps as missing, never invent them. Next: why disaster recovery is more than having backups.

Why disaster recovery is more than having backups

Lesson 4.4 · 2:392 minutes 39 seconds

"We have backups" is the most common answer to "are we resilient?" It's also one of the most dangerous. A backup is a copy. A recovery is a working service, restored within its target, from data you can trust. Three questions separate a copy from a recovery. Is the backup reachable from the same network as the servers? Attackers go after backups first.

Is it clean, from before the intrusion started? Restoring an infected copy brings the attack back. And can you rebuild within the RTO? Rebuilding a platform takes far longer than restoring one file. Falcon Ridge learned this in its risk assessment. Its on-site backups sat on the same network as the servers, so ransomware could encrypt both. The fix, ST-07, is a second copy that is immutable and off-site, tested every quarter.

In an attack, the on-site copy is lost, but the immutable copy survives. Plan for the bad day. Pick a clean recovery point, even if it means losing more data. Restore identity before the applications, once the network is back. And be honest about time: after ransomware, recovering critical systems often takes twenty-four to seventy-two hours, not the four to eight in many older plans.

Falcon Ridge's restore test estimated forty hours for a full rebuild, inside its seventy-two-hour target for backup and restoration. The runbook builds this in. If the primary backups are damaged or may be infected, restore from the immutable copy. Don't restore into the production network until the Information Security Manager confirms it is clean. Then verify the data, and record the restore point and the data-loss window.

Your turn: for your most critical system, answer the three questions honestly. Is a copy unreachable from production? How would you choose a clean point? When did you last test a full rebuild, and how long did it take? Next, in Module 5: exercises that actually change something.
