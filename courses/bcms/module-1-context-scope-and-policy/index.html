Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Before you can protect anything, you have to decide what you're protecting, and why. That's clauses 4.1 to 4.3: the issues that affect your ability to keep going, the people who have requirements, and a scope with clear edges. Section 2.1 lists internal and external issues, but only the ones that affect continuity. Dependence on one hosting site. Key knowledge held by a few people.

For each issue, write the effect, and where it's addressed: a risk in file 03, or a strategy in 04. An issue that leads nowhere is just a note. Falcon Ridge records three. All hosted applications run in one third-party colocation data centre. Two senior agents hold most service desk knowledge. And one internet link carries all cloud traffic. Each links to a risk ID that also appears in its other management systems, so one fact lives in one place.

Next, interested parties and their requirements. Customers want agreed recovery times and notice. Regulators may require incident or breach notification. Staff need safety and clear instructions. Key suppliers owe you notice and recovery support under contract. Keep the laws and contract clauses in a legal register, and review it every year. The scope names which services, which activities and which locations are in.

Falcon Ridge covers three services, six activities, Northport, Westbrook, the colocation site and remote working. And every exclusion has a reason: its security operations pilot is out, because it isn't in steady operation yet, and it's reviewed at the next management review.

Your turn: list three issues that really affect your continuity, four interested parties with their requirements, and write your scope as one sentence: which services, delivered by whom, from where.

Next: the climate change question that every BCMS now has to answer.

In 2024, ISO added a short amendment to many management system standards, including 22301. It's only a few lines long, but certification auditors now ask about it: have you decided whether climate change is relevant to your BCMS? The amendment adds two things. When you look at context, decide whether climate change is a relevant issue. And a note reminds you that interested parties can have climate-related requirements.

It doesn't force you to say yes. It forces you to decide. Record that decision either way, with a reason. It's what the auditor will ask to see. Section 2.2 of the scope document asks three questions. Is it relevant, and why? Does any party have a climate-related requirement? Where is it addressed: which risk, which strategy, and when you'll review it?

Three rows, and the auditor's question is answered. Falcon Ridge says yes. Its summer heat peaks stress cooling and power at the colocation site. That becomes continuity risk BCR-06, scored likelihood three, impact five. It's treated through strategy ST-03, recovery to the cloud environment, with a summer-readiness check and a continuity test each May. If your answer is no, that's allowed, but a no needs a reason just as much as a yes.

For example: our sites and suppliers face no material climate exposure, checked on a given date. Then look at it again at every management review, because exposure changes.

Your turn: answer the three questions for your own BCMS, with a reason and a date. If any customer or regulator has a climate requirement, write it down. Next: the policy, roles and objectives that top management signs.

Business continuity policy, roles and objectives

Lesson 1.3 · 2:322 minutes 32 seconds

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

Clauses five and six turn intentions into commitments. Top management signs a continuity policy, gives people roles with real authority, and sets objectives you can measure. Part B of the scope document holds all three. The policy commits to things an auditor can check. Recover within the times agreed with the business. Base targets on a business impact analysis, reviewed every year.

Record and act on every gap. Exercise the plans on a programme. Plus the legal duties, the resources, and continual improvement. Roles come next. Top management approves and provides resources. The BCMS owner runs it day to day. Activity owners agree the recovery targets for their own activities, which matters later. And the incident management team decides whether to activate the plan, directs the response, and authorises stand-down.

Falcon Ridge's incident management team has six roles: the Chief Executive Officer leads, the BCMS owner deputises, and the heads of infrastructure, end-user services, people, and information security complete it. Roles, not names, so the plan survives a holiday or a resignation. Objectives should be measurable where that's practicable, owned and dated. Falcon Ridge has four: Tier one activities recovered within their RTO in the annual test; every planned exercise held; ninety percent of exercise actions closed on time; and the second internet link in place by the thirtieth of June, to close its known gap.

Your turn: adapt the policy commitments, list your incident management team by role, and write two objectives with a measure, an owner and a date. If you use AI here, use prompt A, and mark any climate decision as an assumption until you confirm it. Next: the business impact analysis.
