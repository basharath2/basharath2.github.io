Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

The BIA tells you what matters and how fast. The risk assessment, clause 8.2.3, asks the next question: what could actually disrupt those activities, how likely is it, and what will you do about it? File 03 starts from six scenario types: loss of a site, an IT platform failure, key people unavailable, a supplier failure, data loss or ransomware, and power or cooling.

Name the activities each scenario hits, using the IDs from the BIA, so the two files stay linked. Score each scenario with one five-by-five method: likelihood times impact. At twelve or more, you treat it. At six or less, the owner can accept it. In between, monitor, and treat it if that's cheap. If you already run an ISO 27001 risk method, use that one instead.

Keep one method. Falcon Ridge assessed six scenarios. Three reach the treat threshold. Failure of the single internet link scores twelve. Ransomware on the servers and the on-site backup, and the summer heat event at the colocation site, both score fifteen. Each treated risk links to a strategy in file 04, and gets a residual score after treatment. Falcon Ridge goes from three risks at treat level to none.

One caution: the internet link's residual score assumes the second link is in place. Until it is, the inherent score still applies.

Your turn: score at least the six scenario types against your prioritised activities. If you use AI, prompt C lists causes and existing controls, but leaves the scores to your risk owners. Next: choosing strategies that actually meet your recovery times.

This is where most continuity programmes quietly fail. A strategy gets written down, but nobody checks whether it can actually recover within the RTO. Clause 8.3, and file 04, make that check impossible to skip. The register offers strategy types that go beyond IT: redundancy and resilience, remote working, replication and failover, an alternate site, a supplier arrangement, backup and restore, spares and stock, and workarounds.

Each option names the resources it needs: people, sites, technology, information and suppliers. For IT, the options form a ladder. Backup and restore is cheapest, and recovers in hours to days. A minimal standby keeps core data replicated and starts the rest on demand. A warm standby runs a scaled-down copy. Active-active runs everything twice, recovers almost instantly, and costs the most.

The old terms, hot, warm and cold sites, sit on the same ladder. Each option records an achievable recovery time, taken from a test or a supplier commitment, not from hope. The register compares it with the RTO from the BIA. Falcon Ridge's current internet link takes eight hours to repair, against a two-hour target, so it shows GAP: recovery six hours over RTO.

Now the test that proves the chain works. Suppose the business tightens application hosting from twelve hours to eight. Paste the new targets from the BIA, and ST-03, which recovers in ten hours, flips to GAP. Nobody types targets into 04. They flow from 02, so a change can't be missed.

Your turn: for each prioritised activity, record at least two options, with an achievable time backed by evidence, choose one, and close or accept every gap with a named owner. Prompt D can list options for you, but it must leave achievable times as to-confirm. Next: single points of failure.

Single points of failure: suppliers and AI services

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

A single point of failure is anything whose loss stops an activity on its own. Some are obvious, like one internet link. The dangerous ones are hidden inside suppliers, and increasingly, inside the AI services your teams have started to rely on. Falcon Ridge has one internet link, contracted with an eight-hour repair time. Today the carrier repairs it, which leaves a gap against the two-hour target.

The fix, a second link from a second carrier with automatic failover, is approved for the end of June. Until then, the gap is recorded, accepted, and owned. Not hidden. The wider version is concentration risk. When one provider sits underneath many of your services, they fail together. And when your supplier relies on someone else, you inherit their single points of failure too.

Ask each critical supplier what they depend on, and how fast they recover. AI has joined that list. In September 2026, three major AI assistants had overlapping outages within about three hours on the same day. For teams that had built AI into customer support, software delivery and automated workflows, that was a business interruption.

So treat AI services like any other critical supplier. Four questions. Map where AI is used, including features hidden inside software-as-a-service tools. Ask what stops if it fails, and put that in the BIA. Plan for automated workflows that stop halfway through a task. And keep a real fallback: a manual process, or a second provider you've actually tested.

Your turn: list your single points of failure, including every AI service a prioritised activity relies on. Give each one a risk in 03, and a strategy, or a recorded acceptance, in 04.

Next: why moving to the cloud doesn't make you resilient by default.

Disaster recovery in cloud computing: shared responsibility

Lesson 3.4 · 2:412 minutes 41 seconds

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

"It's in the cloud, so it's resilient." You'll hear that in almost every continuity workshop. It's half true. The provider keeps its platform running. Whether your service survives a failure depends on choices that are still yours. Cloud providers describe it as shared responsibility. The provider is responsible for the resilience of the cloud: facilities, hardware, network and the platform.

You are responsible for resilience in the cloud: how you spread workloads, your backups, your recovery, and your data, even on managed services. The gap in between, "I thought they did that", is where outages hurt. Most workloads run across zones in one region, so a zone failure is usually survived. A whole region failing needs a second region you designed and tested for.

And the provider itself having a bad day is the case almost nobody plans for. In one 2026 survey, most organisations relied on failover between zones of a single provider. Software as a service is different again: the vendor recovers it, not you.

So in the BIA, name it as a dependency, ask for their recovery time and how they'll notify you, keep an export of your data where you can, and have a workaround. Falcon Ridge does the core of this for its service desk tool: the vendor is named as a supplier, and the workaround is email and phone intake.

Here's a test case from the kit. Falcon Ridge's strategy for identity and email is: rely on the cloud platform's own resilience. On its own, that would be a hope, not a strategy. What made it a strategy is the rest of the row: break-glass accounts in two safes, admin access tested from any location, and an achievable time of four hours against an eight-hour target.

Your turn: for each cloud or software-as-a-service dependency, write down what the provider covers, what you cover, which failures you survive, and your workaround.
