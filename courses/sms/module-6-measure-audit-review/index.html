The KPI register and service reporting

How many of your KPIs have ever changed a decision? Clause 9.1 asks what you measure, how, when, and what you do with the results. Clause 9.4 asks you to report on services. The KPI Register does both, if you use it properly. Twelve KPIs ship, each linked to a clause and an ITIL® practice: availability, incidents, requests, change, problems, SLAs, satisfaction, configuration, continuity and audit follow-up. Each has a shared definition, a direction, target, frequency and owner.

Direction says whether higher or lower is better. The Amber threshold sets where amber turns red. Then RAG calculates itself: green when the target is met, amber between target and threshold, red beyond it. Three examples. Availability: 99.95 percent against 99.9. Green. Mean time to restore: 4.3 hours against a target of 4 and a threshold of 4.4. Amber. P1 incidents resolved in target: 90 percent against 95, threshold 92. Red. Why a threshold per KPI?

Five percent below 99.9 percent availability is about 94.9 percent, so a month with a day and a half of downtime would show only amber. The kit sets availability's threshold at 99.5 percent, still about 3.6 hours of downtime a month. A common mistake is accepting defaults. Decide what "acceptable" means for you. The key test: what would you do if this KPI went red? If the answer is "nothing", drop it. Falcon Ridge's rule: red two months running becomes a management review agenda item, with an owner and an action.

Service reporting shows customers and management the results. Falcon Ridge's monthly report has six parts: SLA performance by service, major incidents, change volume and failures, request fulfilment, satisfaction, and improvement actions. It goes out by the fifth working day, as the SLA promises. Your turn. Keep only KPIs that drive a decision, add any your objectives need, review each Amber threshold, and enter three months of actuals if you have them.

Then draft a one-page service report for last month. Your numbers are flowing. But who checks the system itself? Next: planning and running your first internal audit.

Plan and run an internal audit

Who should find your problems first: you, or the certification auditor? Your first internal audit rehearses certification, and it's required in its own right under clause 9.2. In plain terms: a planned programme checking the SMS meets your requirements and the standard's, and works effectively. Base it on importance and previous results. Use objective auditors, report to management, keep records. A programme is the schedule over time. An audit is one event.

The Internal Audit tab's 26 highest-risk questions make a strong first audit. But over the three-year certification cycle, good practice is to cover all 44 areas. The Audit Programme tab plans high-importance areas yearly, the rest at least once, with a coverage count flagging gaps. Each row gives a question and evidence to sample: ten changes, ten incidents, five configuration items. Sampling turns an opinion into a finding. Auditors note every record they check.

Log yours in the evidence reference column. Grade consistently. A major nonconformity: a requirement isn't addressed at all, or has broken down systemically. A minor: an isolated lapse in a requirement generally met. An opportunity for improvement: you conform, but could do better. In doubt? Ask: would this stop the system achieving its intended outcome? Clause 9.2 expects objective, impartial audits, so nobody audits their own work. Small teams struggle here, so Falcon Ridge cross-audits.

Its ISO 27001 internal auditor audits the SMS, and a service management lead audits parts of the ISMS. Or borrow a trained auditor from the group. Falcon Ridge's first audit, in April 2027, found no majors, two minors, two opportunities. Minors: two of ten sampled changes lacked a post-implementation review, clause 8.5.1. And March's service report went out on working day seven, against an SLA commitment of day five, 8.3.3. Opportunities: one of two agents interviewed couldn't explain the policy, 5.2, and configuration verification only sampled servers, 8.2.6.

Your turn. In the Audit Programme tab, adjust importance, check coverage shows 44 of 44, and name an independent auditor per area. Then run it. Sample real records, grade every row, report to top management. Minors go straight into the nonconformity log from lesson 6.4. Who decides what happens next? Next: management review, where top management decides.

Management review: inputs, outputs, minutes

Does your leadership run the SMS, or just attend meetings about it? Management review is where top management weighs the evidence and decides. Auditors read the minutes for exactly that. In plain terms, clause 9.3 wants planned reviews using defined inputs: actions from previous reviews, changes in internal and external issues, performance trends from KPIs, nonconformities and audits, customer satisfaction, other parties' performance, adequacy of resources, how well risk actions worked, and opportunities to improve.

The template makes that a fixed agenda: one row per input, with a summary, a result and an outcome. Nothing's forgotten, and the auditor can tick them off. The most common weakness? Minutes that only say "the committee noted the report". The outputs that matter are decisions: improvements, changes to the SMS or services, and resources. Every item ends in one of three outcomes: no action, action with an owner and date, or escalation. Falcon Ridge's first review was on 20 May 2027, chaired by the CEO.

SLA targets met: 96 percent, green. Satisfaction: 4.3, green. Changes causing incidents: 5.4 percent, amber. P1 resolved in target: red for the second month, so under lesson 6.1's rule it became a decision: a problem record, owned by the Head of End-User Services, due in July. Audit: two minors, two opportunities, both corrective actions on track. Risk: the secondary internet link went live in April and passed its failover test, so risk R-four was recorded as effectively treated.

Suppliers: the colocation provider's summer-readiness check was complete. Resources: the declared limitation of one ITSM tool administrator was resolved; a second was approved. The security operations pilot stays out of scope until the fourth-quarter review. Five decisions, each with an owner and date. Next review: November 2027. Your turn. Schedule your first review after your internal audit. Use the 07 agenda, send pre-reads a week ahead, and end every item in a decision.

Have the chair approve the minutes within five working days. And those audit minors? Next: handling nonconformities so they stay fixed.

Nonconformity and corrective action

Finding a nonconformity isn't failure. Finding the same one again next year? That is. Clause 10.1 is about making fixes stick. In plain terms, five steps. React: control and correct it, and deal with consequences. Find the cause, and whether it could happen elsewhere. Act on the cause. Check the action worked. Update the SMS if needed, and keep records. Two words auditors care about. A correction fixes this instance: complete the two missing reviews.

A corrective action removes the cause, so it doesn't happen again. Weak nonconformity records stop at the correction. The log: one row per nonconformity. ID, source, clause, description, correction, root cause, corrective action, owner, due date, the effectiveness check's method, date and result, and status. Each column matters. Falcon Ridge's first, from the internal audit: two of ten changes had no post-implementation review. The correction? Complete both.

Root cause? Asking "why" a few times led to the tool: the review task was auto-created only for major changes, not normal ones. So the corrective action added it to the normal change workflow, blocking closure until done. The effectiveness check isn't "workflow updated". It's a sample of ten July changes, all ten showing a review. Two months after the fix, because only time proves a cause is gone. Only then is it closed. The second: March's service report went out late.

Why? One person compiled it by hand, and was on leave. Corrective actions: automate the report, plan action A-one, and name a deputy. Check: the next three reports on time. One nonconformity fed a planned improvement. Your turn. Log every internal audit minor in 07. Write correction and root cause separately, pick a corrective action that removes the cause, and schedule an effectiveness check at least 60 days out. Track "nonconformities closed on time" in the KPI Register.

Then take the Module 6 quiz. Your SMS is built. How do you make it truly yours? One module to go: tailoring everything to your organisation, safely, with AI.
