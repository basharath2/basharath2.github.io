What could stop your SMS delivering, or make it better? That's clause 6.1. Let's build a small, honest risk register. Not a two-hundred-row spreadsheet nobody reads. The standard points to three sources of risk: the organisation, meeting service requirements, and other parties' involvement. Plus opportunities. Most come from work you've already done. Look back. Your context check found climate and skills issues. Your scope named the parties you depend on.

Your SLA chain had weak links, and your readiness checklist had gaps. Each is a candidate risk. Next, one method, used consistently: likelihood times impact, each from 1 to 5. Falcon Ridge treats anything at 12 or more, and accepts 6 or less with the owner's sign-off. Already run ISO 27001? Reuse its method. For an auditor, one method used well beats two used loosely. Falcon Ridge has five risks. R-one: summer heat stresses cooling and power at the colocation site, 12.

R-two: an identity misconfiguration locks everyone out, 10. R-three: two senior agents hold most service desk knowledge, 9. R-four: a single 99.5 percent internet link undermines the 99.9 percent SLA, 12. R-five: an incomplete configuration record leads to wrong change impact assessments, 9. Each risk gets a treatment, owner, due date and effectiveness check. For R-four, it's the secondary internet link from the SLA lesson. For R-one: confirm the provider's cooling redundancy, add a summer-readiness item to the quarterly supplier review, and run a continuity test each May.

Don't skip opportunities. They're part of the requirement. O-one automates the five most common service requests to cut fulfilment time. O-two shares the risk method and document control with the ISO 27001 system. Here's what auditors probe: did the treatment work? A common mistake is actions marked done, and nothing more. Done isn't effectiveness. For R-four, once the failover link is live, test a failover, confirm availability held, and record it.

Your turn. List five to eight risks and at least two opportunities from your context check, scope, SLA chain and readiness gaps. Score them. Anything at 12 or above gets a treatment, owner, due date and effectiveness check. How does it all fit into one document? Next: the SMS plan.

Writing the SMS plan

The SMS plan is where everything connects. Clause 6.3 expects one documented plan for running the SMS. The trick? Write it as a map, not a manual. The plan covers nine headings: objectives, service requirements and known limitations, obligations, roles and authorities, resources, working with other parties, supporting technology, risk handling, and how you'll measure, audit, report and improve. File 06 has a section for each. Most of that already exists: roles in the policy, other parties in the scope, risks in the register, measures in the KPI Register.

The plan points to each in one line, with document IDs. Copied text drifts out of date, and auditors find the mismatch. The most important section: the objectives action table, where clause 6.2's "plan to achieve" lives. Each objective gets an action, resources, owner, due date and evaluation. Falcon Ridge has six. For example, to keep changes causing incidents at 5 percent or less, Falcon Ridge requires a post-implementation review for every normal change from February.

Section 3 is service requirements and known limitations. Be candid. Falcon Ridge notes that one person administers its ITSM tool, and Westbrook field support is two people. To an auditor, declared limitations show they're being managed. Ones the auditor discovers look like surprises. Section 4 lists obligations: the data protection law, national cybersecurity controls, group policies and supplier contracts. Section 8 is the process framework: each SMS process, its owner and key interfaces.

Incident hands over to problem for repeat incidents. Change reads the configuration record. Name the interfaces: that's where processes fail. Finally, the timeline. Falcon Ridge signs scope and policy in January 2027, SLAs in February, then operates and collects evidence from March. First internal audit in April, first management review in May, Stage 1 in June, Stage 2 in August. Four to five months of records before Stage 2. Your turn. Complete file 06.

Write the objectives action table in full, fill the other sections with one-line references, declare at least two known limitations, and set a timeline with at least three months of operation before Stage 2. Already run ITIL® or ISO 27001? Next, the crosswalk shows how much you've already built.

Integrate with ITIL® and ISO 27001 using the crosswalk

Why do the same work twice? Most organisations going for ISO 20000 use ITIL®, and many hold ISO 27001. The crosswalk shows the overlap, so you do it once. Open the Crosswalk tab. Each row maps one requirement area to the ITIL® practice that delivers it, the matching security clause or Annex A control, and a reuse note. First, clauses 4 to 7, 9 and 10. Both standards share one structure, so one document can serve both: context, interested parties, leadership, risk, objectives, competence, awareness, documented information, internal audit and management review.

Falcon Ridge shares one document control procedure and one risk method. That's O-two. Clause 8 is service management specific, so some rows have no match, like service level management and budgeting. Where there is one, like change management and Annex A control 8.32, align the evidence: one change record for both auditors. Security incidents are incidents too. If your incident process covers them, with the security steps in Annex A controls 5.24 to 5.28, one process and one queue serve both.

Now, a common trap. In ISO 20000, 2018, clause 10.1 covers fixing nonconformities, and 10.2 covers improving overall. The 2022 security standard swaps the order. The crosswalk maps them correctly; shared procedures should cite both. The mapping is indicative, for planning. The ITIL® column uses the 34 practice names, reported unchanged in ITIL® Version 5, which regroups them into two families with some content adjustments. Confirm it against your licensed standards.

Your turn. Mark every row where you already have security or ITIL® evidence, add the document ID, and update section 8 of your SMS plan with shared processes. Then take the Module 5 quiz. Your system is planned. Does it work? Module 6 proves it.
