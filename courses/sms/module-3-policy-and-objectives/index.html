Which document does an auditor read first? The policy. It's top management's signature on the SMS, and auditors quote it back all week. So keep it short, true and provable. What does clause 5.2 expect? A policy that fits your organisation's purpose, gives a framework for objectives, commits to meeting requirements and to continual improvement, and is communicated, understood and available. Top management approves it. That's all. It isn't a process manual.

Open file 02. The guidance box says two to three pages. Good. Fill in the document control table. A common mistake? The SMS owner signing as approver. The approver must be top management: the CEO, managing director or equivalent. Section three holds nine commitments: service requirements, the standard, measurable objectives, legal and regulatory obligations, risk, control of other parties, climate and environment, resources, and continual improvement.

Replace the placeholder with your actual laws. In most countries, that typically means a personal data protection law and the national cybersecurity controls, plus any sector rules. Here's where most policies fail. Every commitment is a promise, and auditors ask how you keep it. Treating risks? Your risk register, in Module 5. Controlling other parties? Section six of your scope. Climate change? Your context check. Measurable objectives? The KPI register.

No mechanism? Build one, or drop the commitment. Before signature, run three tests. One: would your CEO actually say this? If it sounds like a template, rewrite it. Two: can staff explain it? Auditors often ask two people what it means to them, so give them one sentence. Three: does every commitment trace to a mechanism? Section five assigns roles: top management, the SMS owner, process owners, service owners and all staff. That's your clause 5.3 evidence, so use real role titles.

Section six covers communication and awareness, section seven exceptions. Section eight sets an annual review at management review. Section nine holds the signatures. A policy isn't in force until it's signed and communicated, so plan both. Your policy promises measurable objectives. But what makes an objective measurable? That's section four, next.

Objectives you can actually measure

Is "improve service quality" an objective? No. It's a wish. Let's make it checkable. Three words get mixed up. The objective is what you want: meet agreed SLA targets. The measure is how you'll know: the percentage of SLA targets met. The target is the level: at least 95 percent. Objective and measure go in the policy. Target and results go in the KPI Register. Why split them? If targets lived in the policy, every target change would need the CEO to re-sign it.

So, framework in the policy, numbers in the register. What does clause 6.2 expect? Objectives consistent with the policy, measurable, monitored, communicated and updated. For each, you plan what will be done, the resources, who's responsible, when it's due, and how results are evaluated. That plan is where most organisations have gaps. When auditors review objectives, it's often the first thing they ask to see. The template gives four areas: service quality, stability, customer and improvement, each with an example objective, measure and owner.

Balanced. What customers get. How safely you change things. How customers feel. Whether the SMS improves itself. Key rule: four to six objectives. With twenty, nobody tracks any, and management review becomes a reading exercise. Each needs a named owner, and data you collect now or can start collecting this month. Link each objective to a KPI Register row. SLA targets met, changes causing incidents, customer satisfaction, and audit nonconformities closed on time are already there.

Set direction, target, frequency and owner, and the RAG status calculates itself. Module 6 covers it fully. Your turn. Choose four to six objectives. For each, write the measure, target, owner, and the one action that will move it this quarter. Can't name the data source? Change the measure. Done properly, what does it look like? Next, Falcon Ridge's completed policy, and how its objectives flow into the KPI Register.

Here's Falcon Ridge's finished policy. Watch three things: specifics, a home for every commitment, and objectives flowing into the KPI Register. The document ID ends POL-001, version 1.0. The owner is the Head of IT Service Management, the approver the Chief Executive Officer. Effective January 2027, next review a year later. It's issued after the scope, DOC-001, because it refers to it. Section two doesn't repeat the scope. It points to it, naming three services and the business units in Northport and Westbrook.

One source of truth. Section three names real obligations: the local personal data protection law and the national cybersecurity controls. The climate commitment matches the context check in Falcon Ridge's scope work. The objectives: four, one per area. SLA targets met, at least 95 percent. Changes causing incidents, 5 percent or less. Customer satisfaction, at least 4.2 out of five. Audit nonconformities closed on time, at least 90 percent. Each has an owner by role, and a matching KPI Register row with the same target.

That traceability is what auditors look for. The roles table has the CEO and Executive Committee approve, resource and chair management review. The Head of IT Service Management is accountable. Process owners, service owners and all staff get one line each. Awareness: the policy goes on the intranet, into induction and into an annual briefing. That briefing is why a service desk agent can answer the auditor. What's missing? How each objective will be achieved.

Falcon Ridge keeps those action plans in its SMS Plan, built in Module 5. That keeps the policy short and covers the plan-to-achieve part of clause 6.2. Your turn. Complete file 02. Name your real laws, and match four to six objectives to KPI Register rows. Run the three policy tests from lesson 3.1, then send it for signature with your scope. Plan a fifteen-minute awareness briefing, then take the Module 3 quiz. You've promised. But what do you deliver? In Module 4, we define it for Falcon Ridge, service by service.
