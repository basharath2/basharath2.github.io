Your scope says which services are in. The catalogue says what each one actually is. Clause 8.2.4 expects it to describe your services, their intended outcomes and dependencies, kept up to date. Open the Service Catalogue tab. One row per service, eleven columns: ID, name, description and outcome, service owner, customers, service hours, support hours, availability target, key dependencies, SLA reference and status. The grey example row is SVC-001, Managed Cloud Workplace.

The description doesn't list mail and chat products. It says staff can communicate and collaborate securely from any location. Lead with the customer's outcome, not your technology. Two columns people confuse. Service hours are when the service should work: here, 24 by 7. Support hours are when people can fix it: Monday to Friday, eight to five, local time. The gap is where incidents wait, so cover it in your SLA. Dependencies list what the service relies on: the cloud workplace tenant, the identity platform, the internet provider and the service desk.

They feed configuration, supplier and continuity work. When one fails, you'll know which services are hit. Status tracks the lifecycle: Pipeline, Live, Retiring or Retired. Only Live services belong in scope and SLAs. A Pipeline service, like Falcon Ridge's security operations pilot, can sit here out of scope. A useful check: the new agent test. Could a new service desk agent, on day one, answer "do we support this?" and "who owns it?" If not, add detail.

If they'd need a technical manual, cut detail. Falcon Ridge adds SVC-002, Service Desk, and SVC-003, Managed Infrastructure. IDs match the scope exactly, and each row points to the SLA we'll write next. Your turn. Replace the example row with your in-scope services, one per row, using your scope IDs. Write outcome-first descriptions of 30 words or fewer, name a person as owner, then run the new agent test. What turns a row into a promise? Next: the anatomy of an SLA.

Anatomy of an SLA

An SLA is a promise with numbers in it. We'll walk the kit's twelve sections, and the one check that stops you promising what you can't deliver. First, how many SLAs? One per service, or per customer group if targets differ. Falcon Ridge writes three. The guidance box sets one golden rule: every target is measured and reported. Can't measure it? Don't promise it. Section 1 names the parties, the service with its catalogue ID, and the term. Section 2 describes it in two or three sentences.

Reuse your catalogue outcome, so the two never drift. Section 3 sets service hours, support hours and maintenance windows. They drive every clock, so be precise, including the time zone. Section 4 is the core: availability, incident response and resolution by priority, request fulfilment and customer satisfaction. Each has a target and a measurement method, which matters as much as the number. "Excluding customer-pending time" should be a rule your tool can apply.

Section 6 lists customer duties. Section 7 commits you to a service report and review cadence: your clause 9.4 evidence. Section 8 lists exceptions. Auditors often read these early: that's where exceptions swallow targets. Keep them honest and short. Section 9, service credits, is optional. Delete it for internal customers like Falcon Ridge's business units; for external clients, align it with the contract. Now, the check most SLAs fail. Every target rests on something beneath: internal teams through operational level agreements, suppliers through contracts.

Promise 99.9 percent availability on a service that depends on an internet link contracted at 99.5 percent, with no redundancy... and you can't deliver. Walk each target down the chain. Fix the weakest link, or fix the target. Finally, sections 10 to 12: changes go through change management, escalation has three levels, and both parties sign. An unsigned SLA is a draft, not an agreement. Where should response and resolution targets come from? Next: the priority matrix.

The priority matrix and response and resolution targets

Priority decides how fast you respond. The clock decides whether you made it. Get either wrong, and SLA reports become arguments, not facts. The kit uses impact times urgency. Impact: many users or a critical function is high, a team is medium, a single user with a workaround is low. Urgency is how fast the damage grows. High and high gives P1; low and low gives P4. Priority is derived, not chosen, so not everything becomes a P1. The template's starting targets, P1 to P4: respond in 15 minutes, 30 minutes, four hours and one day; resolve in four hours, eight hours, three days and five days.

They're placeholders, not recommendations; use your own data. Now the clock. Write three rules into the SLA. One: support hours or calendar hours? Two: does it pause while you wait on the customer? The template says yes. Three: who covers P1s outside support hours? Falcon Ridge runs P1 on-call 24 by 7, so P1 clocks never stop. A P3 is logged Friday at sixteen fifty. Support hours are Monday to Friday, eight to five; the response target, four support hours.

Ten minutes count Friday, the clock stops for the weekend, and the remaining three hours fifty run from Monday morning. Response is due Monday at eleven fifty, not Friday night. If your tool calculates it differently, your reports are wrong. Before committing, pull three months of tickets and see what you really achieve. A sensible first target is one you already meet about nine times in ten; tighten it later through continual improvement. A common mistake?

Aspirational targets, missed monthly, generating nonconformities. Same for requests: the template default is 90 percent within three working days. And customer satisfaction: 4.2 out of five. Match each to the KPI Register and policy objective, as Falcon Ridge does. Your turn. From three months of ticket data, calculate what you achieve per priority, set targets you meet about 90 percent of the time, and write your three clock rules into section 4. What does it look like finished? Next: Falcon Ridge's SLA for Managed Cloud Workplace.

What does a finished SLA look like? Here's Falcon Ridge's, for Managed Cloud Workplace: anatomy, chain and clock, all applied. Customer: all group business units, signed for by the Group Chief Operating Officer. Service: SVC-001, with the catalogue outcome. Service hours, 24 by 7. Support hours, Monday to Friday, eight to five, local time, with P1 on-call 24 by 7. Maintenance: Saturdays, two to six a.m., five working days' notice. Availability: 99.9 percent a month.

Response and resolution use the template's P1 to P4 figures, after checking three months of tickets: P1s were resolved within four hours 91 percent of the time, so the target stays. Requests, 90 percent within three working days. Satisfaction, at least 4.2, matching the policy objective. Now the chain. The platform provider's financially backed commitment is 99.9 percent, so Falcon Ridge can't promise more. Further down, its only internet link was contracted at 99.5 percent.

Rather than lower the target, it added a secondary link with automatic failover, recorded as a risk treatment in the SMS plan. What's not excluded? Platform provider outages count, because business units feel them; they're flagged separately in the service report. Only agreed maintenance and force majeure are excluded. That's the honesty auditors look for. Reports go out by the fifth working day monthly, with a quarterly review per business unit.

Escalation: Service Desk Lead, then Head of End-User Services, then Head of IT Service Management. Section 9, service credits, is deleted: the customers are internal. Your turn. Write the SLA for your most important service: reuse the catalogue outcome, set targets from your baseline, write clock rules, walk the chain for every target, and get both parties to sign. Then take the Module 4 quiz. Foundations built. How will the whole system run? Module 5 plans it.
