Where do you stand?

SMS in Practice · Module 1 · 3 lessons · 8 minutes of video

Score your readiness against clauses 4 to 10, decide what to fix first and plan a two to four week build.

Transcript

Lightly edited for reading; the video is the original.

How ready are you for an ISO 20000 audit, really? Most teams guess. The Readiness Checklist tells you, in under an hour. Open 04, the SMS Workbook, at the Readiness Checklist tab. There are 44 rows, one per requirement area in clauses 4 to 10. Each gives the clause number, a plain-English description of good, and the typical evidence an auditor asks for. At the top: overall readiness as a percentage, and counts of rows implemented, partial, not started and not yet assessed.

Set a status on each row and the figures update. Three statuses. Implemented: documented, operating, and you can show the evidence today. Partial: one of those is missing. It happens but isn't written down, or it's written but not followed. Not started: neither. Here's the key point. If you can't show an auditor the evidence today, it isn't Implemented. Not "we do that". Not "it's in progress". An auditor's first words are usually: show me the record.

Rate on intentions and you get a comfortable score... and a painful Stage 2. How did Falcon Ridge rate three rows? Scope, 4.3: everyone knows the three services, but nothing is signed. Partial. Incident management, 8.6.1: a ticketing tool, priorities and months of records. Implemented. Internal audit, 9.2: never done. Not started. No not-applicable option? That's deliberate. ISO/IEC 20000-1, doesn't let you exclude requirements in clauses 4 to 10.

A common mistake is calling outsourced work not applicable. If a supplier runs, say, capacity management, it's still in scope. Set Operated by to "Other party", and rate how well you control that supplier under clause 8.2.3. Two more columns. Owner: a named person on every row, not a team. Notes: the gap in one line, like "no signed scope, services agreed verbally". Next lesson, those notes become your work plan. Your turn. Rate all 44 rows in one sitting, ideally with a colleague or two who know the reality.

Budget 45 to 60 minutes. Don't research. Rate what you know; flag doubts in Notes. Then note your readiness percentage. But is every gap equal? Next, we put them in priority order.

Score and prioritise your gaps

You've rated 44 rows. Which gaps do you fix first? Most pick whatever feels urgent. There's a better order. Falcon Ridge came out at 57 percent: 18 rows implemented, 14 partial and 12 not started. Don't read too much into that number. It's a baseline, not a grade. What matters is which 26 gaps you close, and in what order. Pass one: foundations. Context and interested parties, scope, leadership, the policy, roles, objectives and document control.

Every other document points back to these. Write an SLA before scope is settled and you'll rewrite it. Tag these P1. They're your first two weeks. Pass two is what this course calls evidence engines: processes an auditor wants to see running over months. Satisfaction measurement, service level reporting, change, incidents, requests, problems, monitoring and service reports. You can't create three months of records the week before Stage 2, and that's where teams are most often caught out.

If any are Partial, tag them P2 and get them producing records now, even if the documents aren't perfect. Pass three is everything else, tagged P3. One-off events, like the first internal audit and management review, you schedule near the end, once there's something to audit. Others, like budgeting, capacity, or availability and continuity, you tackle by effort. Quick wins first. Now make it a plan. Set P1, P2 or P3 in the Priority column, plus a target date on every gap.

The summary counts open gaps per priority. P1 dates fall in weeks one and two. P2 processes should produce records by week three. Finally, filter the Priority column on P1. That short list is your to-do list for Modules 2 and 3. For Falcon Ridge, it's six rows, led by the unsigned scope and a policy never approved. Your turn. Set every gap's priority and target date, and send the P1 list to whoever will sign your policy. Top management's attention is itself a clause 5 requirement. Use it. So how do these priorities become a two to four week build path?

Your 2–4 week build path

You know where you stand and what to fix first. How do the next two to four weeks fit together? Read Me First gives a nine-step order of work. We'll group it into four weeks. Part-time, alongside the day job, most teams need all four. With a dedicated person, two is realistic. Week one: foundations. Readiness is done. Next, fill in the master context in the AI Prompt Pack. Fifteen minutes now saves hours later; Module 7 covers it. Then draft your scope statement in Module 2.

Everything depends on scope, so get it signed this week if you can. Week two: direction. Write the policy and objectives, modelled on Falcon Ridge, and get top management to approve them. Then build your service catalogue. The SLAs hang off it. Week three: agreements and plans. Draft an SLA for each customer-facing service, set KPI targets linked to your objectives, and write the SMS plan with its risks and opportunities. Keep your P2 evidence engines producing records.

Week four: check and review. Run your first internal audit with the workbook, raise nonconformities, and hold a first management review. Use Module 7 to tailor anything that still reads like a template. Then comes what no template can do: running the system. Most organisations operate for a few months, collecting incidents, changes, reports, audit results and review minutes, before Stage 2. When auditors assess a new SMS, those records are what they want to see.

Your turn. Book four one-hour blocks a week for the next four weeks, and name who approves the scope and the policy. Then take the Module 1 quiz. Module 2 answers the question everything depends on: what exactly does your SMS cover? See you there.
