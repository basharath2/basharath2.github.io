Scope the SMS

What's the most important page in your SMS? The scope statement. It decides what the auditor looks at, and it's printed on your certificate. A scope has three dimensions: which services, delivered by which organisational units, from which locations. Build it around services, not departments. Customers buy services, the certificate names services, and every other document in the kit, from the catalogue to the SLAs, is organised by service. First, spend ten minutes on context in Annex A, at the back of the template, because your scope must take it into account.

List the internal and external issues affecting your services: growth, skills, regulation, technology changes. Then list your interested parties and what they require: customers, regulators, suppliers, top management. Since the 2024 amendment, add one more question. Is climate change a relevant issue for your services, and do interested parties have climate-related requirements? For many IT providers in hot climates, the answer is yes. Extreme summer heat strains data centre cooling and power, hitting availability and continuity.

Some customers now ask for energy or emissions reporting. Annex A has a short table for this. Relevant? Record it and where it's handled. Not relevant? Record why. Either way, you're ready when the auditor asks. For more depth, ISO published TS 20000-16, in 2025: guidance on sustainability within a 20000-1 SMS. Now open file 01, the SMS Scope Statement. Start with document control: ID, owner, approver, and a review date no more than 12 months out.

Sections 1 and 2 are one sentence each: who you are, where you're based, what you deliver, and to whom. Section 3 is the heart. One row per service: a service ID, its customers, and where it's delivered from. Reuse those IDs in the catalogue. If a service isn't in this table, it isn't in scope. Section 4 lists the units and locations delivering those services, including remote support and cloud-hosted delivery if they apply. Auditors reviewing a scope often ask: where is this service really delivered from?

Section 5 is the one-line scope for the certificate: your organisation, the services, the customers, the locations. Good practice: make it say exactly what the table says. No more, no less. Brivellis recommends starting narrow. Certify three services well and extend next year, rather than scope everything and fail on the weakest. Next: what most scopes get wrong. Interfaces, other parties and exclusions

Who actually delivers your services? Almost never just you. Cloud platforms, data centres, a parent company's network team. So who do you depend on, and what can you leave out? First, the key distinction. You can limit your scope: fewer services, units or locations. You can't exclude requirements. Inside your scope, every requirement in clauses 4 to 10 applies. "We're excluding problem management" isn't a valid scope. It's a nonconformity waiting to happen.

Section 6 covers other parties in the service lifecycle: anyone who provides part of a service or runs part of a process for you. External suppliers, internal teams outside the scope, even a parent company. For each, say what they provide and how you control it. Here's the principle behind clause 8.2.3. Handing over work doesn't hand over accountability. If your hosting provider runs capacity management, you must still show capacity is managed, through a contract, targets and regular reviews.

When auditors check this, the "how it is controlled" column is what they test. Section 7 records what's out of scope, and why. Services, units or locations, never requirements. Give a real reason, like "a pilot service, not yet in steady operation", or "managed separately under a different management structure". A vague reason invites the auditor to question the whole boundary. Section 8 covers other management systems. Running ISO 27001? Say what you share, like the risk method or document control.

Auditors like to see sharing done deliberately. Five common mistakes. One: scoping by department, not service. Two: excluding a process instead of limiting the scope. Three: leaving suppliers out of section 6. Four: certificate wording that doesn't match the services table. Five: no signature from top management. Check your draft against each. Finally, sections 9 and 10. The scope is reviewed at least yearly at management review, and whenever services, locations or units change.

Top management approves and signs it. An unsigned scope is only a draft. Next: Falcon Ridge's scope statement, then yours.

What does a finished scope look like? Here's Falcon Ridge IT Services, with the last two lessons applied. Falcon Ridge is the shared IT services company of a group headquartered in Northport. Three services are in scope, each with an ID it reuses everywhere: Managed Cloud Workplace, Service Desk and Managed Infrastructure, all for the group's business units in Northport and Westbrook. Four units deliver them: the Service Desk, IT Operations, Infrastructure Engineering and the Service Management Office.

Locations: the Northport operations centre, field support in Westbrook, remote support, and a colocation data centre in Northport run by a third party. Section 6 gets serious. Four other parties: the cloud workplace platform provider, the colocation provider, a telecoms carrier, and Group Finance, an internal team outside the scope. Each has a control. The colocation provider has a contract with an SLA, a quarterly review and an annual site visit.

That's what an auditor would test. Two things are out of scope. Managed Security Operations is a pilot with two business units, not yet in steady operation, due for review at the next management review. Hardware procurement is a purchasing activity run by Group Procurement, not a managed service. Both are services left out. Neither is a requirement left out. Falcon Ridge also ran the context check. Climate change is relevant: summer heat peaks strain the colocation site's cooling and power, and the group's sustainability office now asks for annual energy-use reporting.

Both are recorded, and return in the SMS plan and the availability and continuity work. Here's the certificate line: one sentence, matching the table exactly. Your turn. Take 45 minutes. Do the context check, climate question included, then fill in file 01, sections 1 to 10. Check it against the five mistakes, and send it to your top-management approver. Practising? Add a fourth Falcon Ridge service and decide if it belongs in scope. So what will top management sign? Module 3: policy and objectives.
